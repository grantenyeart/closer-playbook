The Film Room

Real closing calls, broken down minute by minute. The playbook tells you the seven phases. This page shows you what they sound like when they work, and what it costs when they don't.

How to use this: do not watch a whole call front to back. Read the breakdown first, then jump to the timestamps that matter. You will learn more from six targeted minutes than from a full recording.

01

Grade every call on this

Seven rows, one per phase. Score each out of 2: 0 did not happen, 1 attempted, 2 executed. Fourteen is a perfect call. Anything under 9 and the deal was probably lost before the pitch.

PhaseWhat you're looking forScore
1. ControlOwned the first 60 seconds. Set the agenda. Got them talking about why they showed up./ 2
2. DiscoveryMade them say the numbers out loud. Current, goal, and the gap between them./ 2
3. BudgetGot their number before quoting yours. Knows what they already spend and on what./ 2
4. PitchSold the outcome, not the feature list. One product matched to the pain they named./ 2
5. The AskAsked for the deal. Asked again. Stopped talking after asking./ 2
6. ObjectionsIsolated before answering. "Is it the money, the timing, or something else?"/ 2
7. CommitmentLeft with a date and a time on the calendar. Never "I'll reach out."/ 2
The talk-time rule

Before you score anything, ask one question: who talked more? On a good discovery call the prospect does 60 to 70 percent of the talking. If the closer did most of it, phases 2 and 3 did not really happen no matter what the scorecard says.

02

Teardown 1 · The deal that was won twice and lost anyway

Divine Detailing Work Logan Lamb · Greensboro, NC June 11 · 27 min Closer: Grant Result: Lost ▶ Recording

Scorecard: Control 2, Discovery 2, Budget 2, Pitch 0, Ask 1, Objections 0, Commitment 0. 7 of 14. The front half of this call is close to perfect. The back half gives all of it away.

Why this call is worth 20 minutes of your time

The prospect said the offer was a no-brainer in the first thirty seconds. He said yes to buying at 14:48. He confirmed again at 17:32. And he still did not pay. Almost nobody loses a deal this cleanly, which is exactly what makes it teachable.

What went right
  • 0:14Opened by making the prospect sell himself. "What made you want to hop on this call? What about our offer stood out to you?" Logan answered "the percentage I'd be losing per job isn't terrible, and the amount of jobs I'd be getting, it's kind of a no-brainer." Never pitch to a man who just told you he's already sold.
  • 2:50Got current, goal, and gap out loud. "What revenue are you sitting at per month, and what's the goal?" $4K now, $6K target. Then he said the gap back: "So about $2,000?" That is phase 2 executed exactly as written.
  • 3:12Ran a business audit before the call. Knew the Instagram had 5,000 followers and complimented the video quality specifically. Preparation reads as respect.
  • 4:06Chained the CRM diagnostic. Do you track leads, what software, can you mass text. Three questions, each narrowing. Found a real hole instead of assuming one.
  • 8:13Screen shared instead of describing. Pulled up Matrix Mobile Detailing live, showed the promo, the ceramic page, the memberships. Demo, don't debate.
  • 20:58The best moment in the call. "How many leads did he bring you last month?" Two bookings. Then "Do you pay him up front?" $950 a month. He surfaced that this man is burning $950 a month for two jobs.
What cost him the deal
  • 4:33He talked roughly three quarters of a 27 minute call. The stretch from 8:13 to 13:57 is nearly six unbroken minutes of The closer. A doctor asks and listens. This became a lecture.
  • 4:33He talked the buyer out of the thing the buyer already wanted. Logan came in pre-sold on lead gen. He said the competition was too heavy and pivoted to website and software instead. This is the fatal move. He replaced a yes with a harder sell.
  • 6:00He pitched three products. Website, then software, then Google ads later. By 13:57 Logan could not have told you what he was being asked to buy.
  • 21:36He never used the $950 anchor. Logan is paying $950 a month for two bookings. Our lead gen costs nothing up front. That comparison was sitting right there and never got made.
  • 14:48He got a yes and kept talking. "If it's going to help get me where I want to go, I don't see why not" is a yes. So is "I get what you're saying, a hundred percent" at 17:32. Both times he kept selling instead of collecting.
  • 17:37He put a monologue between the yes and the invoice. Forty seconds about the owner being busy and needing payment first, right after a man told him he'd been burned by agencies.
  • 18:16He never isolated the objection. "Time-wise right now that wouldn't work out best for me" went unexamined. Nobody asked whether it was the timing or the money.
  • 24:39He ended with "I'll reach out." No date, no time, no next call. Phase 7 of our own playbook says never do this, and it is exactly how this ended.

The exact 90 seconds where it was lost

Grant"So the way that's kind of like, are you looking to get that software in place?"
Logan"I mean, if it's going to help and get me to where I want to go, then I don't see why not."
Grant"Yeah, 100%. I can set you up a call. The software, it's just $300 a month..."
Stop right there

That was the sale. The correct next sentence is nine words long: "Perfect, what's the best email for the invoice?" Instead the call ran eleven more minutes, and in those eleven minutes Logan talked himself back out of it.

The $950 he left on the table

What The closer pitched

$300/month software, on top of the $950/month Logan is already paying an agency that produced two bookings.

Total ask: $1,250 a month from a man doing $4,000 a month.

What was available

"You're paying $950 a month for two bookings. We don't charge anything up front, you pay per qualified lead. What would you rather have that $950 doing?"

Same product he came in pre-sold on. Funded by money he's already losing.

Sell them the thing they already said yes to. Then stop talking and collect.
One thing that has changed since this call

Grant pitches Meta ads for ceramics at 3:00 and again at 22:00. We no longer sell Meta. That partnership ended. The equivalent today is Google Pay Per Click. Ignore that thread when you watch.

03

Teardown 2 · The one the playbook was built on

Summit Auto Detail Michael Strickland · Asheville, NC June 15 · 57 min Closer: Grant Result: Closed ▶ Part 1 ▶ Part 2

Scorecard across both parts: Control 2, Discovery 2, Budget 2, Pitch 2, Ask 2, Objections 2, Commitment 2. 14 of 14. Closed all three offers: Google lead gen, Meta setup, and the AI receptionist. $1,500 up front, Meta setup split into four payments.

The headline: he bought the thing he said he refused

At 15:25 Michael says "I don't want AI. I do not want AI. I refuse to do that. That's garbage." At 52:08 he pays for the AI receptionist. Thirty seven minutes to completely reverse a stated belief, and it happened for one reason.

What won it
  • 0:32Risk reversal in the first thirty seconds. "We show companies what we do before we ask for a dime." Said to a man who opened the call complaining about getting ripped off. He led with the exact thing the prospect was afraid of.
  • 1:14Got the competitor's price at minute one. "How much were they charging you?" $1,000 a month plus ad spend for a promise of 10 coatings. Then: "That's how they cook you, man. Our pricing, you'll probably like the sound of it compared to that." He anchored against their number before ever saying his own.
  • 2:24Discovery until the prospect named the gap himself. Needs 15 details a week. Two vans. $3,500 a week now. Wants $5,000. Then the confirming question: "the biggest bottleneck is not workers, it's jobs coming in?" Michael did most of the talking through this whole stretch.
  • 6:43Derived the price from their ticket, not a price sheet. "What would you quote me for an interior exterior?" $250 to $350. "We'll meet in the middle at $300, so we ask $30 per valid lead." The fee came out of the prospect's own numbers.
  • 8:41Held steady through open disbelief. "That doesn't make any sense, I can't fathom what you're saying. How is that worth it for you?" He explained the real economics, including that they used to charge 20% and lowered it. No defensiveness, no backpedaling.
  • 15:30Answered the AI objection with a question. "Have you ever experimented with AI at all?" Not a rebuttal. He dug into the bad experience first, then reframed the product as a gatekeeper that only passes high intent calls through. Michael: "Yeah, I kind of like that."
  • 40:00Then he played the demo live. Called the AI on speaker and let it handle a price objection and a vomit cleanup. Michael: "I didn't realize that it could overcome objections and actually pin down a price and pin down a day." This is the moment the deal turned.
  • 45:55Discounted for the bundle, not for the pressure. "The receptionist is normally a thousand. If you're getting the meta ads and the Google ads, I'll do 50% off to implement all three." The discount bought a bigger deal instead of shrinking one.
  • 36:55Allowed the downsell but priced the delay. Michael asked to skip the $1,000 Meta setup. "We could set it up that way. The longer you wait, you're missing deals. How quickly do you want to get to that 5K a week?" Handed the choice back tied to the prospect's own goal. Michael took all three anyway.
  • 52:35Collected on the call. "I can send it, you ready?" Square link and agreement forms out before hanging up.
What still needs fixing
  • 35:52The prospect asked his name 36 minutes in. "What's your name?" Which means there was never a proper introduction. Phase 1 is control, and control starts with the prospect knowing who they're talking to.
  • 28:42"What's the name of your company?" Nearly half an hour before Michael knew he was talking to Rapid Reach. Same gap as above.
  • 13:06Long tangents. Weather, a stolen-car background check story at 29:04, the grape and watermelon analogy at 39:09. Rapport is good and this was a lot of it inside 57 minutes.
  • 55:43Ended without money actually collected. "I do need to talk to you real quick, but I will get back to you ASAP." The forms went out but the call closed on "you'll hear from me shortly." It landed, but that is a gap, not a plan.
  • 45:29The partner objection got one attempt. He offered a joint call with the wife, Michael declined, and it was dropped. Worth one more try: "what's the one number she'll want to see?"

Part 2 · the paperwork call, and how a commitment actually sounds

Twenty six minutes, same day. This is the half nobody records and everybody needs, because the deal is won and it can still fall apart here.

What held the deal together
  • 2:00He defended the discount by re-anchoring, not by caving. Michael tried to renegotiate to 10% on any lead he closed himself. Grant: "Our typical average is 20% on the meta side. I knew because you were getting the full setup, I was giving you the discount of 15." Michael answered his own objection: "I would give you the margin, but I don't have any. So we're just at the 15 then." Never defend a discounted price. Restate the undiscounted one.
  • 5:30He refused to promise something he couldn't back. Michael pushed hard for a guarantee at $35 a day. Grant held: "We don't do guarantees because it fluctuates from place to place. But based on our averages you'll be seeing three to four a week." The guarantee exists only at $50 a day, and he did not blur that line to get a signature.
  • 8:00He removed the money objection with structure, not discount. Four payments on the Meta setup, offered the moment it was asked for. Price never moved.
  • 24:00The commitment, stated as a sequence. "Look through those, get those signed. Once you get those signed and sent back, I'm going to send you the payment plan for the meta ads. And then I'll send you the upfront payment for the AI." Michael: "Sounds good. Let's go." Everyone knows what happens, in what order, and who does it.
  • 20:00He survived a demo that broke live. The AI had been switched to gatekeeper mode at the client's end and refused to quote. He named what happened, tied it back to the gatekeeper idea Michael already liked, and moved on. No panic.
What nearly cost him the momentum
  • 10:00The paperwork turned into a mess. Agreements went to the wrong address (summitautodetailing instead of summitautodetailavl), then got resent three separate times across several minutes of dead air. Michael eventually asked: "Can you just bulk email all the agreements in one email? This is a little much."
  • 10:00The fix is thirty seconds of prevention. Read the email address back out loud before you send anything, and send one email with every agreement in it. Admin friction at the signature is where momentum dies.
  • 24:00Still no time attached. "Give me a text whenever you send those agreement forms over." The sequence is clear but the clock belongs to the prospect. The upgrade: "I'll watch for those tonight. If I haven't seen them by tomorrow afternoon I'll give you a ring, that alright?"
The lesson from the pair

Part 1 sold him. Part 2 is where he read every line of three agreements, tried to renegotiate the rate, pushed for a guarantee, and asked for a payment plan. The close is not the end of the selling. Every one of those was handled without moving the price.

Note before you watch

A large part of this call pitches Meta ceramic ads, which we no longer sell. Everything about how the objection was handled still applies. Just substitute Google Pay Per Click wherever you hear Meta.

Teardown 3 · The upsell that was sitting there for months

Nine Lions Mobile Car & Truck Wash Arthur Holt · Memphis, TN Aug 31 · 60 min Closer: Grant Existing client · monthly report + upsell Result: Pending, no date set ▶ Recording

Scorecard: Control 2, Discovery 1, Budget 1, Pitch 1, Ask 2, Objections 2, Commitment 0. 9 of 14.

Why this one is different, and why you need it

This is not a new-business close. It is a monthly report on an existing client with an upsell attached, and you will run far more of these than cold closes. The client is already paying, already happy, and the entire job is to widen the account.

What worked
  • 0:33Opened on their wins, screen shared, numbers first. 242 leads against 140 last month, a 72% jump, beating competitors 98.93% of the time. You earn the right to upsell by proving the current spend worked.
  • 1:50Congratulated the review growth and then asked how he did it. "What did you notice that helped bring in that volume?" Making the client narrate his own win is worth more than praising him.
  • 20:47He pushed back on the math and Grant rebuilt it on the spot. Arthur: "That's not a realistic number for me, my detail is not $300, I'm $200." Grant recalculated to $12,400 immediately instead of defending the original figure. Never defend a number the client has already rejected. Rebuild it in their units.
  • 41:26The question that unlocked the whole call. "Have you heard our AI receptionist before?" No. Then he played it live, and it handled a price objection on tape. Arthur: "That is insane." Then: "You are a hell of a damn salesman, that hit all the damn points that folks try to get over on."
  • 56:37He asked permission before challenging the stall. "Do you mind if I challenge you on that for a second?" Arthur said "absolutely, go ahead." Permission turns a confrontation into a conversation.
  • 57:00Then he shrank the risk to one number. "Out of those 62 leads, you'd only need five to convert at $200." Big investment reframed as a small, obviously beatable threshold.
What it cost
  • 41:26The biggest miss on this page. He had been pitching this AI for months and had never once demoed it. Arthur went and found a $40/month competitor, because with nothing to compare he was shopping on price alone. The demo took two minutes and flipped him completely. Demo on the first pitch, not the fifth.
  • 4:42He argued the missed-call number instead of acknowledging it. Arthur twice said he answers his phone. By 5:27 he was clipped: "anyway, that doesn't matter, you say 62, let's move on." That is irritation, not agreement. Say "fair enough, could be tracking oddly, either way here's what it's worth" and move.
  • 11:18He could not answer a direct pricing question about his own account. Arthur asked what the retainer would be if he put his own card on file. "I don't know those numbers off the top of my head, I'll have to talk to Ryan." He asked twice. On a report call with a paying client, know your own numbers or say exactly when you will have them.
  • 17:23The client did the math and found 25%. Arthur computed $1,600 on $6,305 and said "we're averaging 25%, not 10." The answer given was to raise his prices. That is a deflection and he noticed. Address the number he found, then talk pricing.
  • 1:00:00No date, again. It ended on "send it to me in an email" and "five or six weeks." He said out loud what would convert him, and that is a buying signal, but nothing went on a calendar.
The one lesson

If you have a demo, lead with it. Months of describing the AI produced a man shopping $40 alternatives. Two minutes of hearing it produced "that is insane." You cannot argue someone into value, and you do not need to when you can just let them hear it.

The close that was available and not taken

Arthur handed over the script at 53:55: "the best way to convert me now, if you could write this down and send it to me in my email, you probably have a high success rate with me." A client telling you how to sell him is the strongest buying signal there is. The move was to send it during the call and book the follow-up before hanging up, not to promise an email and accept a six week delay.

Phase Library

The fastest way to learn one move is to watch it run several ways back to back. Jump straight to these timestamps.

Phase 1 · Control

Summit 0:32  Risk reversal before anything else. "We show companies what we do before we ask for a dime."
Divine 0:14  Making the prospect sell himself. "What about our offer stood out to you?"
Counter-example: Summit 35:52, the prospect asks his name 36 minutes in. Introduce yourself.

Phase 2 · Discovery

Summit 2:24 to 4:47  The full arc. Volume, capacity, daily target, weekly revenue, then the goal. Prospect does the talking.
Divine 2:50  Current to goal to gap in three questions. $4K, $6K, "so about $2,000?"

Phase 3 · Budget

Summit 1:14  The competitor's price inside the first two minutes, then used as the anchor.
Summit 6:43  Deriving our fee from their ticket. "What would you quote me?" then 10% of the middle.
Counter-example: Divine 20:58, the $950 was found and never used.

Phase 4 · The Pitch

Summit 5:22  Matched to the bottleneck the prospect named, with proof at his scale.
Summit 45:55  The bundle. Discount tied to taking all three, not to pressure.
Counter-example: Divine 4:33, talking the buyer off the product he already wanted.

Phase 5 · The Ask

Summit 52:35  "I can send it, you ready?" Then the Square link, on the call.
Counter-example: Divine 14:48, a clear yes followed by eleven more minutes of selling.

Phase 6 · Objections

Summit 15:30  "I refuse AI" answered with a question, not a rebuttal.
Summit 20:58  The gatekeeper reframe that made the refused product acceptable.
Summit 40:00  The live demo. The single highest leverage 3 minutes in either recording.
Summit 36:55  Downsell request allowed, with the cost of waiting attached.
Summit Part 2, 2:00  Holding a discount. Rate renegotiation answered by restating the undiscounted 20%. The prospect talked himself back to 15%.
Summit Part 2, 5:30  Refusing a guarantee he could not back, and keeping the deal anyway.

Phase 7 · Commitment

Summit Part 2, 24:00  The model to copy. The commitment stated as a numbered chain: you sign, then I send the payment plan, then I send the AI invoice. The prospect knows exactly what happens next, in what order, and who owns each step.
Summit 53:36  Agreement forms sent while still on the call, not after it.
The one upgrade left: attach a clock. "Give me a text whenever" hands the timing to the prospect. Say instead: "I'll watch for those tonight. If I haven't seen them by tomorrow afternoon I'll give you a ring, that alright?"
Counter-example: Divine 24:39, "I'll reach out." No sequence, no owner, no time.
